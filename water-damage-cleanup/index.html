The First 48 Hours After the Water Stops

Forty-eight hours is the working window for most cleanup decisions. Within that time, wet carpet pad turns sour, drywall starts to sag and damp upholstery begins to smell. A same-day crew, when one is available, can often save materials that would be lost after a long wait.

Cleanup is not the same as drying. Cleanup removes and cleans. Drying equipment, covered under water mitigation, removes moisture from what remains. Most jobs run both at once, so the crew can pull a soaked pad while the air movers start on the floor.

What to Do Before Cleanup Starts

  • Stay out of rooms where the water came from a drain, toilet or outdoor source until a crew has assessed them.
  • Photograph soaked items before anything is moved or thrown out.
  • Wear gloves and boots when handling wet debris, and bag it separately.
  • Do not run a household vacuum on water or use heat to speed drying.

Sanitizing and Deodorizing

Sanitizing matters most when the water carried dirt or sewage. ANSI/IICRC S500 sorts water into three categories by contamination level, and the category decides how much is cleaned in place and how much is removed. Crews wash hard surfaces with an antimicrobial cleaner matched to the surface, vacuum with a HEPA filter and wipe studs and joists before any new material goes up.

Odor is handled at the source. Spraying a fragrance over a soaked pad hides the smell for a day. Removing the pad, cleaning the floor beneath it and drying the room is what ends it. If a smell lingers after drying, a second look for hidden moisture usually finds the reason.

Saving Belongings and Papers

Contents come second to people and structure, but they carry the most personal value. Hard goods such as glass, metal and sealed plastic wash up well. Books, photographs and paper records are fragile, and freezing wet papers in sealed bags stops damage until a document specialist can dry them.

Pack-out moves contents to a dry space so the crew can work on the room. Ask what is being moved, where it goes and how it is listed, because the same list helps with your insurance inventory.

Shops, Offices and Shared Buildings

Water in a retail unit or office suite, in a place like the Ferncroft office park or a shopping center on Independence Way near the Liberty Tree Mall, follows the same cleanup sequence as a house. The difference is who owns what. Walls, ceilings and the roof often belong to the landlord, while stock and fixtures belong to the tenant.

Ask your landlord and your insurer who handles each part before any demolition starts, and photograph every affected shelf and fixture.

Insurance and Cleanup Records

Your insurer will ask what was lost, so keep a written list of removed items with photos taken before disposal. Hold a few samples of discarded material when the adjuster asks to see them. Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered, gradual leaks and neglect often are not, and flood damage needs its own policy.

Ask your insurer what applies to your loss, and save every invoice, including disposal and cleaning supplies.
